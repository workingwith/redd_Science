Chapter 1: Earthquakes and Earthquake Waves

Earthquakes happen along faults in Earth's crust. They send out waves of energy, and how strongly a place shakes depends on distance and on what is built there.

Key terms

FaultA break in Earth's crust where rocks slide past each other. Earthquakes happen along faults.
Focusextra The point underground where an earthquake's rupture begins.
EpicenterThe point on Earth's surface directly above the focus. Damage is usually strongest here, which is why it matters to rescuers.
Body wavesSeismic waves that travel through Earth's interior: P-waves and S-waves.
Surface wavesSeismic waves that travel along Earth's crust: Love waves and Rayleigh waves.
MagnitudeThe size of an earthquake at its source. It is a fixed number.
IntensityThe strength of shaking people feel in different places. It changes depending on distance and on how sturdy nearby buildings are.
TsunamiA large, powerful ocean wave that can form after an earthquake under the ocean floor.

Faults: three ways rock breaks and moves

Body waves and surface waves

Body wavesSurface waves
Travel throughEarth's interiorEarth's crust (along the surface)
ExamplesP-waves, S-wavesLove waves, Rayleigh waves
MotionP-wave: push-pull. S-wave: side-to-side.Rayleigh wave: rolling motion.
SpeedP-wave is the fastest wave; it arrives first.Arrive after the body waves.

Magnitude versus intensity

  • Magnitude is the size of the earthquake at its source. It does not change from place to place.
  • Intensity is the strength of shaking people feel. It can be different in different places, partly because of distance and partly because of how sturdy the buildings are — a country with weaker buildings can experience higher intensity from the very same earthquake.
  • A higher magnitude number means far more energy is released. This quiz's answer key teaches that a magnitude 7 earthquake releases about 32 times more energy than a magnitude 5 earthquake.

Earthquakes and coasts

  • An earthquake under the ocean floor may cause a tsunami that strikes the shore.
  • A place closer to the epicenter usually shakes more strongly than a place farther away.

Chapter 2: Volcanoes and Weathering

Volcanoes bring heat and melted rock up from inside the Earth. Once rock is at the surface, weathering slowly breaks it down in two different ways.

Key terms

VentThe opening that runs from the magma chamber up to the top of the volcano.
Craterextra The bowl-shaped opening at the top of a volcano, where the vent reaches the surface.
Magma chamberextra The underground pool where melted rock collects beneath a volcano.
Shield volcanoA volcano with gently sloping sides, built from thin, runny lava.
Pyroclastic flowA fast, burning cloud of hot ash, gas, and rock from an explosive eruption. More dangerous than a lava flow because it travels quickly and burns everything in its path.
Ring of FireThe chain of volcanoes that circles the edge of the Pacific Plate.
WeatheringThe breaking down of rock over time.
Physical weatheringBreaks a rock into smaller pieces without changing what it is made of.
Chemical weatheringChanges the minerals in a rock, changing what it is made of.
Ice wedgingWater seeps into a crack, freezes, and expands, splitting the rock. A type of physical weathering.
OxidationA chemical reaction with oxygen, such as a metal turning reddish-brown (rust). A type of chemical weathering.

Inside a volcano

  • A shield volcano has gentle slopes because its lava is thin and runny.
  • Volcanologists (who study volcanoes) and seismologists (who study earthquakes) often work together — for example, monitoring volcanic tremors to warn communities of a possible eruption.

Types of eruptions, from quietest to most violent

  1. Hawaiian
  2. Strombolian
  3. Vulcanian
  4. Pelean
  • Hawaiian: quiet eruption; produces a fountain of lava that runs down the sides.
  • Strombolian: a relatively quiet eruption with mild, repeated bursts.
  • Vulcanian: a violent eruption that sends lava, ash, cinders, and gas into the air.
  • Pelean: the most violent type; produces an avalanche of red-hot ash and gases racing down the slope.

Watch out: Pyroclastic flows (as in a Pelean eruption) are far more dangerous than slow lava flows because they travel quickly and burn everything in their path.

Weathering: physical vs. chemical

Physical weatheringChemical weathering
What changesSize and shape of the rock onlyThe minerals the rock is made of
ExamplesIce wedging, abrasion, burrowing animals, plant roots splitting rockOxidation (rusting), acid rain reacting with rock, formation of speleothems (cave mineral formations)
  • A tree's roots growing into a rock's cracks and splitting it apart is physical weathering.
  • Acid rain reacting with the minerals in a marble monument is chemical weathering.
  • An old iron gate slowly turning reddish-brown outdoors is oxidation, a chemical process.
  • Valleys, cliffs, and canyons are mostly shaped over long periods by rain, ice, and snow breaking down rock.

Chapter 3: Natural Resources

People use fossil fuels, minerals, and other energy resources from the Earth. Some of these can be replaced by nature fairly quickly; others cannot.

This chapter's questions come from the school's own quiz, but the copy given did not include a completed answer key. The answers below are standard, accurate science — please check them against the teacher's key if you have it.

Key terms

Fossil fuelA fuel formed when the remains of plants and animals are buried quickly and change underground over a very long time. Coal, petroleum, and natural gas are fossil fuels.
MineralA solid substance found naturally in the Earth.
UraniumA mineral used to produce nuclear energy.
RefineryA factory that separates crude oil into different products.
Renewable resourceA resource nature can replace in a relatively short time, such as soil or hydroelectric energy.
Nonrenewable resourceA resource that takes an extremely long time to form and cannot be quickly replaced, such as coal or petroleum.
MalleableAble to be hammered, dented, or shaped without breaking — a property of many metals.
Geothermal energyEnergy from the heat inside the Earth.

Renewable or nonrenewable?

RenewableNonrenewable
Soil, hydroelectric energyPetroleum, coal, natural gas, uranium (and other minerals)

A nonrenewable resource cannot be replaced by nature in a relatively short period of time — it takes an extremely long time to form.

Energy resources: advantages and disadvantages

Wind & solar energy: shared disadvantage

Both need large areas of land. Wind energy also depends on a steady, dependable wind supply.

Geothermal energy: an advantage

The hot water it produces can be used for recreation, such as hot springs.
  • Hydroelectric energy is not a fossil fuel — it comes from moving water.
  • Malleable metals can be dented or shaped without breaking.

Chapter 4: Cells and Organisms; the Microscope extra

This chapter did not have a quiz yet, so it uses standard, accurate Grade 6 facts about cells and microscopes.

Key terms

CellThe basic building block of all living things.
UnicellularMade of only one cell, such as an amoeba or a bacterium.
MulticellularMade of many cells working together, such as humans, animals, and plants.
Cell membraneThe thin covering that controls what enters and leaves the cell. Found in every cell.
Cell wallA rigid outer layer found in plant cells, but not in animal cells. Gives the cell extra support and shape.
NucleusThe cell's control center. Holds the cell's genetic material and directs its activities.
CytoplasmThe jelly-like substance inside the cell where the other parts float.
MitochondriaThe organelle that releases energy for the cell to use.
ChloroplastFound only in plant cells. Captures sunlight and makes food for the plant.
VacuoleA storage sac for water, food, or waste. Plant cells usually have one large vacuole.
