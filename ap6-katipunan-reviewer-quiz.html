Lesson 1 — The Katipunan

Founding. Andres Bonifacio founded the Katipunan on July 7, 1892, after the exile of Jose Rizal convinced him that peaceful reform had failed. Its goal was complete, absolute independence from Spain through armed revolution.

Key leaders.

  1. Andres Bonifacio — founder and Supremo (leader) of the Katipunan
  2. Emilio Jacinto — called the "Brains of the Katipunan"; wrote the Kartilya, its code of conduct
  3. Pio Valenzuela — a key member who later met with Rizal in Dapitan

The four governing councils.

CouncilLevel it governed
Kataas-taasang Sanggunian (Supreme Council)The entire Katipunan — its highest authority
Sangguniang Bayan (Popular Council)A single town or municipality
Sangguniang Balangay (Provincial Council)An entire province
Secret CouncilBonifacio, Jacinto, and Valenzuela; acted as judges over members who broke the rules

Membership ranks, lowest to highest.

  1. Katipon — password: "Anak ng Bayan"
  2. Kawal (soldier) — reached by recruiting new members
  3. Bayani (patriot) — the highest rank

Protecting the members.

  • Triangular Method: each member knew only two others, so the Spanish could not uncover the whole network if one member was caught.
  • Blood Compact: a new member signed his name in his own blood as a symbol of total loyalty.

The Katipuneras (women members). Gregoria de Jesus, Marina Dizon, Josefa Rizal, and Trinidad Rizal were among the first women to join. They kept documents and secrets safe, helped recruit members, organized meetings disguised as social gatherings, and smuggled important documents.

Kalayaan. The official newspaper of the Katipunan. Its writers used pen names so the Spaniards could not identify them.

Core values expected of members: loyalty, unity, responsibility, and love of country.

Lesson 2 — The Philippine Revolution Against Spain

Rizal vs. Bonifacio. Jose Rizal favored preparation and gradual reform, while Andres Bonifacio believed the time for reform had passed and pushed for immediate armed revolution.

Timeline of events.

DateEvent
Aug 19, 1896The Katipunan is discovered by Spanish authorities, forcing the movement into the open
Aug 21, 1896Katipuneros move to and stay at the house of Apolonio Samson in Kangkong
Aug 23, 1896Cry of Pugad Lawin — at the house of Juan Ramos, Katipuneros tear up their cedulas as an act of defiance, formally starting the armed revolution
1896Pio Valenzuela travels to Dapitan to inform Rizal of the Katipunan's plans and ask his advice
Dec 30, 1896Jose Rizal is executed; his death strengthens Filipino anger and inspires more support for the revolution
March 1897Tejeros Convention — held to organize the revolutionary government; Emilio Aguinaldo is elected president
1897Pact of Biak-na-Bato is concluded

The Cavite problem: Magdalo vs. Magdiwang. The revolution in Cavite split into two rival factions — Magdalo (associated with Emilio Aguinaldo) and Magdiwang (associated with Mariano Álvarez). Their disagreement over leadership and authority divided the revolutionaries.

Republic of Biak-na-Bato. A revolutionary government established during the struggle against Spain; it led to the Pact of Biak-na-Bato, a truce with the Spanish colonial government.

Correct sequence to remember: Cry of Pugad Lawin → Rizal's execution → Tejeros Convention → Pact of Biak-na-Bato.

Lesson 3 — The End of Spanish Colonialism in the Philippines

Background. By 1898, Spain was also at war with the United States (the Spanish-American War). Emilio Aguinaldo, who had been in exile in Hong Kong after the Pact of Biak-na-Bato, returned to the Philippines with American support to continue the revolution against Spain.

Battle of Manila Bay (May 1, 1898). Commodore George Dewey led the American fleet to a decisive victory over the Spanish fleet in Manila Bay, crippling Spain's naval power in the Philippines.

Declaration of Philippine Independence (June 12, 1898). At Aguinaldo's home in Kawit, Cavite, the Philippines declared independence from Spain. The Philippine flag was waved publicly for the first time, and the Marcha Nacional Filipina (our national anthem, composed by Julian Felipe) was played publicly for the first time. Ambrosio Rianzares Bautista read the Act of the Declaration of Independence.

Malolos Congress and Constitution. A congress of Filipino representatives convened in Malolos, Bulacan in September 1898 to draft a constitution. The Malolos Constitution, approved in January 1899, was the first republican constitution in Asia, and it established the First Philippine Republic, with Emilio Aguinaldo as its first president.

The Mock Battle of Manila (August 13, 1898). The United States and Spain secretly agreed to stage a battle so that Spain could surrender to the Americans instead of to the Filipino revolutionaries, preserving Spanish honor. Filipino forces were kept out of the city.

Treaty of Paris (December 10, 1898). Spain and the United States signed the Treaty of Paris, ending the Spanish-American War. Spain ceded the Philippines, along with Puerto Rico and Guam, to the United States, and was paid $20 million for the Philippines. This treaty formally ended over 300 years of Spanish colonial rule in the Philippines (1565–1898) — though Filipinos had no part in the negotiations that decided their country's future.
